UMDAC Datathon 2026 · Swift case · Team Nai Long · working analysis

How the Swift payment generator works, rule by rule

We profiled all 10,000,000 synthetic payments (30 Apr – 31 Jul 2023) and reverse-engineered the generating logic. Some rules reproduce the data exactly, some are strong statistical fits, and a few stay open. Each rule below gives its condition, effect and scope, the chart or calculation behind it, a real-world reading, and how far to trust it.

00 · What is being judged

The task is to reconstruct causal rules, not to describe the data

Swift asks us to recover the business rules used to build the dataset. The data is a directed temporal pseudograph of banks and parties, flattened into a table. Judges reward precise rules (condition → effect → size → scope), causal checks over plain correlation, and honest partial or negative results. A rule marked uncertain loses fewer marks than a wrong one stated with confidence.

02 · Feature group: time (EPOCH_SECONDS)

Payments follow the sender's local office clock, scaled by a global weekly multiplier

T1

Intraday profile runs on the sending bank's local time

Exact
local_hour = UTC_hour + offset(country of ORDERING_BIC8) volume ramps at 07:00 local, plateaus 07–20h with peaks at 07h and 16–18h, and drops to ~1%/h at 01–05h

Observed: In UTC, every country's profile is the same curve shifted in time. Aligning each country to GB recovers whole-hour offsets that match time zones (US −5, DE +1, CN +8, JP +9). Reading: people and back offices start payments during their working day, so cut-off-driven traffic clusters after opening and before end of day.

T2

Offsets are frozen at January (no daylight saving)

Strong
offset = UTC offset on a northern-winter date: US −5 (EST), GB 0 (GMT), DE +1 (CET), AU +11 (AEDT), NZ +13 (NZDT)

Observed: In May–July, real offsets would be US −4, GB +1, AU +10. The data shows the winter values in the north and the summer values in the south. Reading: a generator artefact (offsets computed once at a fixed date). It is a checkable, non-obvious rule, and it changes local hours by one hour for DST countries.

T3

One weekday shape everywhere: weekends at roughly 20–35% of a weekday

Exact
local weekday share: Mon 18.9% · Tue 17.0% · Wed 17.0% · Thu 18.0% · Fri 18.9% · Sat 3.6% · Sun 6.5% (identical within ±0.5pt across all 60+ countries, including Gulf and Israeli banks)

Observed: the shape does not depend on the sending country. Sunday is busier than Saturday everywhere. Rejected: "Friday–Saturday weekend in Middle-East countries" (AE, SA, IL look identical to US). Reading: batch submission before the Monday window. Local weekend customs were not modelled.

T4

Weekly volume is a global step multiplier; nothing else moves with it

Partial
weekly volume ≈ base × {1.5, 1.0, 1.0, 1.7, 1.7, 1.5, 1.7, 2.4, 1.9, 1.0, 1.2, 1.5, 1.9} (weeks from 1 May) all countries move together (±5%); amounts, party mix, routing and duration stay flat across weeks

Observed: the step levels are shared by every country, and the weekday shape inside each week is unchanged. Not explained: why these weeks. Peaks in late May, the week of 19 June (2.4×) and late July hint at month and quarter-end flows, but 1–7 May is also high, so we mark the cause as uncertain.

03 · Feature group: currency & FX (CURRENCY_CODE, CURRENCY_CONVERSION_COUNT)

Senders pay in their home currency; FX counts every currency border along the route

C1

Payment currency is the sending bank's home currency about 89% of the time

Strong
P(currency = home currency of ORDERING_BIC8 country) ≈ 0.84–0.95 per country (overall 89.4%) otherwise a vehicle currency: EUR 464k · USD 231k · GBP 78k · CHF 58k · CNY 30k · SGD 23k the home-currency share does not depend on party type (89.3–89.6%) and barely on amount

Observed: each country has one dominant currency (EUR for the euro area; GBP for IM and JE; CHF for LI). RU stands out at 69% RUB. Reading: customers fund payments from local-currency accounts. Invoices priced in global vehicle currencies (EUR, USD) take the rest, in proportion to how widely those currencies are used.

C2

FX count = number of currency borders between consecutive banks

Exact · 9,999,998 / 10,000,000
CURRENCY_CONVERSION_COUNT = Σ over adjacent known BICs (i, i+1) in BIC8_PATH of 1[home_ccy(i) ≠ home_ccy(i+1)] '...' placeholders are skipped; the payment's own currency is NOT part of the rule

Observed: the rule matches every row except 2, whose BIC country has no currency in our map. Rejected: "count distinct currencies" (90.6%) and "add 1 if payment currency ≠ first bank" (93.5%). Reading: each correspondent converts into the currency of the account it holds with the next bank, so FX steps follow currency borders.

04 · Feature group: routing (TRANSACTION_TYPE, BIC8_PATH, PARTICIPANT_COUNT, CONTINUITY_QFLAG)

Route length is a coin-flip chain with a 1/e continuation probability

R1

Number of intermediaries K = ⌊Exp(1)⌋

Exact fit
P(K = k) = (1 − e⁻¹)·e⁻ᵏ → P(Direct) = 0.632, and each extra hop is 0.368× as likely as the previous one PARTICIPANT_COUNT = K + 2 = number of known BICs in the path

Observed: the ratios between successive counts are 0.3676, 0.3680, 0.3671, 0.3685… The Direct share is 0.632 ± binomial noise for every sending bank and the same for domestic and cross-border payments. Rejected: "routing depends on the bank pair": only 10% of payments sit on pairs that always use one type, which is chance level. Reading: in the generator, a correspondent relationship exists with a fixed probability at each step. In reality this would depend on bilateral account networks.

R2

Covered is a constant 11.3% of non-direct routes

Exact fit
ttype = Direct if K=0, else Covered with p ≈ 0.113 (independent of K, party type, domestic/cross-border), else Indirect

Observed: the Covered share is 11.32%, 11.29%, 11.33% at K = 1, 2, 3. Covered routes are slower (median 288 s vs 87 s at pc = 3) and slightly larger (+0.15 in ln amount). Reading: the cover method (MT103 sent direct, MT202 COV through correspondents) adds a second settlement leg.

R3

Intermediaries are drawn in proportion to bank size

Strong
P(bank b chosen as intermediary) ∝ activity(b); corr(log intermediary uses, log payments sent) = intermediaries sit in the payment-currency zone only 17% of the time

Rejected: "intermediaries are clearing banks of the payment currency" (for example USD through US banks). Reading: large banks (US, CH, GB, DE hubs) act as correspondents because they hold the most nostro accounts.

R4

Each link hides with 5% probability, and gaps chain geometrically

Exact fit
P(CONTINUITY_QFLAG = N) = 1 − 0.95^(pc − 1); per hidden link, extra '...' tokens ~ Geometric(0.05) CONTINUITY_QFLAG = 'N' ⇔ path contains '...' (100%)

Observed: predicted vs actual: 5.00/4.99%, 9.75/9.74%, 14.26/14.25%, 18.55/18.50%. Gap counts on direct paths fall 299,417 → 15,076 → 739 → 30, a ratio of 0.05 each step. Reading: legs outside the observed messaging channel (for example a domestic clearing leg) leave unobserved hops.

05 · Feature group: processing time (TOTAL_DURATION)

Duration is a sum of per-bank processing times, set by each bank's straight-through rate

D1

Each bank adds either a fast (seconds) or a manual (~20 min) step

Strong
TOTAL_DURATION ≈ Σ over participants b of { fast step ≈ 7–15 s with prob STP(b) ; manual step, median ≈ 1,400 s, otherwise } both banks fast: median 27 s · one slow: ~1,400 s · both slow: 2,666 s (≈ 2 × 1,400)

Observed: log-duration is bimodal (peaks at 16–32 s and 512–2,048 s). The slow mode adds up across slow banks. The lower quantiles grow by about 7–8 s per extra participant. Reading: straight-through processing versus manual repair or compliance review at a bank.

D2

STP rate is a bank attribute, and small banks are far more often manual

Strong
share of banks with slow-rate > 90%: <100 payments 55% · 100–999 55% · 1k–9.9k 41% · 10k+ 0.8% no dependence on hour, weekday, party type or amount (slow share 39–43% across amount deciles)

Observed: per-bank slow shares are bimodal (about 0.05–0.2 or about 1.0), far wider than binomial noise. Rejected: "queued outside business hours" (slow share flat across 24 h) and "large payments get reviewed" (slightly the reverse). Reading: large banks invest in STP automation, while small banks key payments by hand.

Two-bank payments split into a seconds mode and a ~20-minute mode

Slow banks add their delays: one slow bank ≈ 1,400 s, two ≈ 2,700 s

06 · Feature group: payment value (USD_AMOUNT)

Amounts are log-normal, and each business party multiplies the typical value by e² ≈ 7.4

A1

An additive log-mean model by party type

Exact fit
ln(USD_AMOUNT) ~ Normal( μ = 6 + 2·1[ordering = B] + 2·1[beneficiary = B] + country_o + bank , σ ≈ 2.0 ) observed μ: CC 6.06 · BC 7.99 · CB 8.03 · BB 10.00 ; σ = 2.28–2.29 in every segment (skew −0.12)

Observed: the four segment means sit on an exact 2-unit lattice with identical spread. Medians: C→C $464, B→C $3,118, C→B $3,236, B→B $22,883. Reading: commercial flows (supplier and payroll) are an order of magnitude larger than personal remittances, and business-to-business trade is the largest.

A2

The sending country sets a wealth level; the beneficiary country barely matters

Strong
country_o effect (ln units): MC +2.0 · LI +1.7 · LU +1.2 · KY +1.1 · IE +1.0 … IN −2.6 · BD −3.2 ; sd across beneficiary countries ≈ Variance of ln amount (5.22): country · bank · account · noise

Observed: the effect correlates with country payment volume (r = 0.49). Individual accounts add almost nothing. Reading: the case study's "GDP" style rule: financial centres and rich economies originate larger tickets. Country values are randomised, so the ranking is not real-world.

A3

Round amounts are slightly more common between businesses

Partial
share of multiples of 1,000 (normal rows): BB 2.7% · BC 1.9% · CB 1.8% · CC 0.9%; integers 6.9–7.7%

Detected but not modelled further. It may be a by-product of how the amount is generated in the original currency before conversion.

07 · Feature group: parties, accounts & details text

Text formats follow SWIFT MT conventions and country rules; beneficiary location is decoupled

P1

Party type sets the name grammar, never mixed

Exact
type B → <business> 86%, <company_keyword> 26%, never first/last name type C → <last_name> 86%, <first_name> 58%, never business tokens P(ordering = B) = 78.7% (varies by country 38–96%) ; P(beneficiary = B) = 28.8%, independent of ordering type

Reading: corporate senders dominate cross-border initiation, while beneficiaries are mostly individuals (payroll, suppliers paying sole traders, remittances).

P2

Account format: IBAN only in IBAN countries, about 92% of the time

Exact
IBAN-country banks: 84–100% IBAN ; non-IBAN countries (US, CN, AU, JP, SG, CA, HK, IM, JE…): 0% IBAN country prefix = BIC country in 100% of rows

Mirrors the real IBAN registry. The structured 50F/59F address layout (1/ 2/ 3/) is also set per country: never in LI, MC, LU, QA; always in IM, JE, CK; about 15% elsewhere.

P3

Free-text fields obey the 4×35 MT layout

Exact
optional '/account' line + ≤4 lines of ≤35 chars (99.96% of lines ≤35; 99.83% ≤4 lines); <line_break:35> marks forced wrapping

The generator wraps names and addresses into the fixed-width fields of an MT103 message.

P4

Senders live where they bank; beneficiaries usually do not

Strong
ordering address country = BIC country: 100% ; beneficiary address country = BIC country: 0.98% beneficiary address country is weighted toward populous countries (CN, BR, UA, ET, IN, MX…) while beneficiary banks cluster in hubs (US 19%) each beneficiary account keeps one address country (100%)

Reading: the generator samples the beneficiary's residence by population and their bank by banking size, independently. In the real world this would mean diaspora or non-resident accounts. Here it is almost certainly a modelling shortcut, and we label it as such.

P5

Account activity is heavy-tailed

Strong
391k sending accounts; 1,216 accounts with ≥1,000 payments make 29.5% of all payments; 2.59M beneficiary accounts (median 1 payment) each account has a single type and a single bank (100%)

Large corporates and payment aggregators send the most, while individual receivers appear once or twice.

08 · Feature group: anomalies (ANOMALY_TYPE, 7,006 rows = 0.07%)

Each anomaly is injected with its own signature: sequence, copy, scaling or mule account

X1

Romance scam: escalating bursts from consumers

Exact fit
500 series, one consumer → one beneficiary, 2–8 payments each (counts 61, 95, 64, 71, 74, 66, 69) each payment ≈ 1.5× the previous one (Δln ≈ +0.40 per step); gap between payments mean ≈ 306 s (median 215 s) sender is a consumer in 100% of rows; amounts small (median $109); senders concentrated in IN, CN, US, BR, ID, PK, NG

Reading: victims are groomed into repeated, growing transfers. The case text says C→C, but the beneficiary type follows the base mix (27% B), so the rule constrains only the sender.

X2

Duplicate payment: 2–5 identical copies within 2 hours

Exact
185 groups (sizes 2/3/4/5 → 37/43/49/56 groups); all copies share sender, beneficiary, amount, currency, path AND duration span ≤ 7,043 s; EVERY copy is labelled, including the first

Reading: a back-office resend or race condition replays the same message. The labelling convention matters: a detector cannot treat the first copy as clean.

X3

Fat finger: amount ×100 (77%) or ÷100 (23%)

Strong
log₁₀(amount / expected) is bimodal at about +2.3 and −2.3; up-share = ; party mix and timing look like normal traffic

Reading: a decimal separator (comma vs dot) misplaced by two digits. We cannot tell ×100 from ×1000 for individual rows because normal amounts already vary by ±0.9 in log₁₀.

X4

CEO and account-replacement scams pay into mule accounts at small, slow banks

Strong
CEO: sender = business in 100%; cross-border 98.7%; amount +1.4 ln above the segment norm both: ~48% of beneficiary accounts receive ONLY scam money, each from several victims (up to 10) beneficiary bank median size ~720 payments vs 8,050 normally; 45% are slow (non-STP) banks vs 17%

Observed: the scams' longer durations (median 741 s and 571 s vs 56 s) come from which bank is involved, not from an added delay: within the same bank-speed stratum, scams are no slower. Negative result: we found no evidence that account-replacement scams reuse a legitimate payee's name with a swapped account (1 match in 649).

X5

Customer recall: indistinguishable from normal traffic

Open
317 rows; party mix, amount, duration, routing and banks all match the normal profile

We found no signature in the observable fields. A recall is a post-hoc event (the customer asks for the money back), so it may be labelled at random by design. Reported as a negative result.

09 · Limits

What we could not explain yet

  • Weekly multipliers (T4): the levels are measured, but we have not found a cause. Next test: correlate with calendar features (month-end, quarter-end, 4-4-5 patterns).
  • Vehicle-currency choice (C1): which non-home currency is picked. It looks like global currency popularity, but this is not proven.
  • Fast-step size (D1): per-hop fast time looks Gamma-like at about 7–15 s; we have not fitted the exact distribution or the manual-step tail (max 21,649 s).
  • Bank-level amount effect (A2): about 0.22 of ln-variance sits at bank level. Is it a bank attribute or bank-size driven?
  • Country B-share (P1): ranges from 38% to 96% and is unrelated to the amount effect (r = −0.18); its origin is unknown.
  • Customer recall (X5) and the account-replacement name-swap hypothesis: no signal found.
10 · Professional lenses

What each discipline takes from these rules

Statistician

Prefer exact-match tests where a rule is deterministic (C2, R4) and distribution fits where it is stochastic (R1 geometric, A1 log-normal). Report match rates, ratios and variance shares rather than p-values: at n = 10M everything is "significant".

Actuary

Fraud is a frequency–severity problem. Frequency is 7 per 10k payments. Severity varies: romance scams are small but repeated and compounding (×1.5 per step); CEO scams are large single losses (+1.4 ln). Mule concentration means a few beneficiary accounts carry the aggregate exposure.

Finance & economics

Home-currency invoicing with EUR and USD as vehicles, FX at every currency border, correspondent hubs in US, CH and GB, and the cover method adding settlement time all match how correspondent banking works. Tie these to the "Interpretation" marks (12%).

Data scientist / AI engineer

A rules-first approach beats black-box models here, because judges score explicit rules. ML is useful only for discovery (for example a tree on dur > 120 to confirm bank identity dominates). Detectors: duplicate = exact-key match within 2 h; romance = escalating burst; mule = beneficiary that only receives anomalous money.

Software engineer

4.6 GB CSV → Parquet (1.27 GB) → slim Parquet (0.48 GB) in DuckDB; every figure is regenerated by one script. Disk was nearly full (4 GB free), so no intermediate database was kept.

MBA / Swift strategy

For Swift's network: STP gaps at small banks drive delay (an investment case for gpi-style tracking), hidden legs (7.7%) reduce transparency, and anomaly signatures (bursts, duplicates, mule beneficiaries) can be detected from network data alone.
